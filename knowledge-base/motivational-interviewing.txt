Motivational Interviewing (MI)

Motivational Interviewing is a collaborative, person-centered counseling style developed by William Miller and Stephen Rollnick. It is designed to strengthen a person's own motivation and commitment to change by exploring and resolving ambivalence. It is widely used for addiction, health behavior change, and any situation where a person is stuck between wanting to change and not wanting to.

The core insight: people are more persuaded by what they hear themselves say than by what others tell them. MI helps people articulate their own reasons for change.

---

THE SPIRIT OF MI

MI is not a set of techniques applied to a client — it is a way of being with another person. The spirit has four elements:

Partnership: The counselor works with the person, not on them. The person is the expert on their own life.

Acceptance: Unconditional positive regard for the person. This includes affirming their worth, honoring their autonomy (their right to choose), and empathizing accurately with their experience.

Compassion: Actively promoting the person's wellbeing and prioritizing their interests.

Evocation: The motivation and resources for change already exist within the person. The task is to draw them out, not install them.

---

AMBIVALENCE

Ambivalence — wanting and not wanting change at the same time — is normal and not a sign of weakness or resistance. Most people can see both sides: "I know drinking is hurting my relationship, but it's the only way I know how to unwind." MI treats ambivalence as the starting point, not an obstacle.

The righting reflex: When someone expresses ambivalence, helpers often feel the urge to argue for change (the "righting reflex"). This backfires — it activates the person's counterargument. MI works in the opposite direction: explore both sides, evoke the person's own reasons for change.

---

THE FOUR PROCESSES OF MI

1. Engaging: Building a working relationship. Without trust and connection, nothing else works. Use open questions, reflections, and affirmations.

2. Focusing: Developing a clear direction. What does the person want to work on? Stay focused on the change goal without imposing an agenda.

3. Evoking: Drawing out the person's own motivation for change. This is the heart of MI.

4. Planning: Moving toward a concrete commitment and plan when the person is ready.

---

CORE SKILLS: OARS

Open questions: Invite elaboration rather than yes/no answers.
"What brings you here today?" "What concerns you about your drinking?" "What would your life look like if things changed?"

Affirmations: Genuine recognition of the person's strengths, efforts, and values. Not empty praise.
"You've been carrying a lot and you're still here." "It took courage to talk about this."

Reflections: Reflect back what the person has said to show understanding and invite deeper exploration. The most important skill in MI.
- Simple reflection: repeat or rephrase what was said
- Complex reflection: reflect the deeper meaning, emotion, or implication
"It sounds like part of you really wants things to be different." "You're worried about what people will think, and at the same time you're tired of hiding this."

Summaries: Periodic summaries collect and link what has been said, emphasize change talk, and invite the person to add or correct.

---

CHANGE TALK AND SUSTAIN TALK

Change talk: statements that move toward change. Types (DARN-CAT):
- Desire: "I want to..."
- Ability: "I could..."
- Reasons: "It would help me..."
- Need: "I have to..."
- Commitment: "I will..."
- Activation: "I'm ready to..."
- Taking steps: "I've already started..."

Sustain talk: statements that move away from change. "I'm not sure I can." "I've tried before and failed." "It's not that bad."

MI involves selectively reflecting and elaborating on change talk and responding softly to sustain talk. When sustain talk arises, reflect it without reinforcing it and gently turn attention back toward change.

---

ROLLING WITH RESISTANCE (DISCORD)

When the person pushes back, MI counselors do not argue back. Instead:
- Reflect the resistance without judgment: "It sounds like you're not sure this is a problem."
- Double-sided reflection: "On one hand you want to keep things as they are, and on the other you're here because something doesn't feel right."
- Shift focus: move away from the stuck point to something less charged
- Reframe: offer a different interpretation of what the person has said

Arguing for change while the person argues against it is always counterproductive.

---

THE IMPORTANCE OF READINESS

MI recognizes that readiness to change varies. The Stages of Change model (Prochaska and DiClemente) offers a framework:
- Precontemplation: not yet considering change
- Contemplation: aware of the problem, considering change, but ambivalent
- Preparation: making plans to change
- Action: actively changing behavior
- Maintenance: sustaining the change

Intervening at the right stage matters. Pushing action-stage strategies on someone in precontemplation creates resistance and damages the relationship.

---

MI AND THERAPEUTIC ALLIANCE

Research consistently shows that the quality of the therapeutic relationship is one of the strongest predictors of outcome in therapy. MI's emphasis on empathy, collaboration, and evocation directly builds this alliance. Even when MI techniques are not used explicitly, the spirit of MI — respecting autonomy, expressing genuine empathy, and avoiding the righting reflex — improves therapeutic relationships across all modalities.
