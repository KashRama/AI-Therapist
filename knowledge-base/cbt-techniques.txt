Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) Techniques

CBT is based on the idea that our thoughts, feelings, and behaviors are interconnected. By identifying and challenging negative thought patterns, we can change how we feel and act.

Thought Records
Thought records help identify and challenge automatic negative thoughts. When feeling distressed, write down: the situation, the emotion and intensity (0-100%), the automatic thought, evidence for and against the thought, and a balanced alternative thought. This process slows down reactive thinking and introduces objectivity.

Cognitive Restructuring
Cognitive restructuring challenges distorted thinking patterns known as cognitive distortions:
- All-or-nothing thinking: seeing things in black and white with no middle ground
- Catastrophizing: assuming the worst possible outcome will happen
- Mind reading: believing you know what others think without evidence
- Overgeneralization: drawing broad conclusions from a single event
- Emotional reasoning: assuming something is true because it feels true
- Should statements: rigid rules about how you or others must behave
To challenge these, ask: What is the evidence? What would I tell a friend? What is the most realistic outcome?

Behavioral Activation
Depression often leads to withdrawal and inactivity, which worsens mood. Behavioral activation involves scheduling pleasant and meaningful activities to break this cycle. Start small — even a 10-minute walk or calling a friend counts. Track mood before and after activities to build awareness of what improves wellbeing.

Grounding Techniques for Anxiety
The 5-4-3-2-1 method anchors attention to the present moment:
- 5 things you can see
- 4 things you can physically feel
- 3 things you can hear
- 2 things you can smell
- 1 thing you can taste
This interrupts anxious thought spirals by engaging the senses.

Progressive Muscle Relaxation
Systematically tense and release muscle groups for 5-10 seconds each, moving from feet to face. This reduces physical tension associated with anxiety and stress. Practice daily for best results, not just during moments of distress.

Exposure Therapy
Gradual, repeated exposure to feared situations reduces anxiety over time. Build a fear hierarchy from least to most anxiety-provoking, then work through it step by step. Staying in the situation until anxiety naturally reduces (without using safety behaviors) is key to learning that the feared outcome does not occur.

Problem-Solving
When overwhelmed, structure helps. Steps: define the problem clearly, brainstorm all possible solutions without judgment, evaluate pros and cons of each, choose the best option, implement it, and review the outcome. This reduces helplessness and builds confidence.

Mindfulness in CBT
Mindfulness involves observing thoughts and feelings without judgment, rather than getting caught up in them. Thoughts are not facts — they are mental events that come and go. A simple practice: sit quietly for 5 minutes, focus on the breath, and when the mind wanders, gently return attention to breathing without self-criticism.
