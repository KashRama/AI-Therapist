Dialectical Behavior Therapy (DBT) Techniques

DBT was developed by Marsha Linehan originally for borderline personality disorder, but is now widely used for emotional dysregulation, self-harm, eating disorders, depression, and PTSD. It blends CBT with acceptance strategies drawn from mindfulness and Buddhist philosophy. The core dialectic is balancing acceptance ("you are doing the best you can") with change ("you need to do better").

DBT is organized into four skill modules: Mindfulness, Distress Tolerance, Emotion Regulation, and Interpersonal Effectiveness.

---

MINDFULNESS SKILLS (the foundation of DBT)

Mindfulness in DBT means observing and participating in the present moment without judgment. It is the core skill that underlies all other modules.

What Skills (what to do):
- Observe: notice thoughts, feelings, and sensations without getting caught up in them. Just watch.
- Describe: put words to what you observe. "I notice a feeling of tightness in my chest."
- Participate: fully engage in the current activity without self-consciousness.

How Skills (how to do it):
- Non-judgmentally: drop evaluations of good/bad, right/wrong. Stick to facts.
- One-mindfully: focus on one thing at a time. Bring the mind back when it wanders.
- Effectively: do what works, not what is "right" or "fair." Focus on goals, not winning arguments.

---

DISTRESS TOLERANCE SKILLS

Distress tolerance skills help survive crisis moments without making things worse. They are not meant to solve problems — they are meant to get through unbearable moments.

TIPP (for crisis moments when emotion is overwhelming):
- Temperature: change body temperature to quickly reduce emotional arousal. Holding ice, splashing cold water on face, or dunking face in cold water activates the dive reflex and slows heart rate within seconds.
- Intense exercise: burst of physical activity burns off stress hormones. Even 20 jumping jacks can reduce acute distress.
- Paced breathing: slow exhale longer than inhale. Breathe in for 4 counts, out for 6-8. Activates the parasympathetic nervous system.
- Progressive muscle relaxation: systematically tense and release muscle groups.

ACCEPTS (distraction from crisis):
- Activities: engage in something absorbing
- Contributing: help someone else — shifts focus outward
- Comparisons: compare to a harder time you survived, or to others facing more difficult challenges
- Emotions: create a different emotion (watch a funny video, listen to uplifting music)
- Pushing away: mentally set the problem aside temporarily
- Thoughts: replace crisis thoughts with other thoughts (count, recite, puzzle)
- Sensations: use intense but safe sensations (hold ice, taste something sour, smell something strong)

Self-Soothe with the Five Senses:
Deliberately comfort yourself through sight, sound, smell, taste, and touch. Examples: light a candle, listen to calming music, wrap in a soft blanket, drink herbal tea, look at nature. This activates the soothing system.

Radical Acceptance:
Pain is inevitable; suffering is optional. Suffering = pain + non-acceptance. Radical acceptance means fully accepting reality as it is, not as you wish it were. This does not mean approving of it. It means stopping the fight against what cannot be changed. Signs of non-acceptance: "It shouldn't be this way," "I can't stand this," repeatedly thinking about how unfair something is.

IMPROVE the Moment:
- Imagery: visualize a peaceful place or a successful outcome
- Meaning: find or create meaning in the difficulty
- Prayer: connect to something larger than yourself
- Relaxation: use one relaxation technique
- One thing in the moment: bring attention fully to the present
- Vacation: take a brief mental or physical break
- Encouragement: be your own cheerleader ("I can get through this")

Pros and Cons:
When urges to engage in harmful behavior are strong, write out the pros and cons of tolerating distress versus acting on the urge. This activates the rational mind.

EMOTION REGULATION SKILLS

The goal is not to eliminate emotions but to reduce vulnerability, increase positive emotions, and change unwanted emotions.

ABC PLEASE (reducing emotional vulnerability):
- Accumulate positive experiences: short-term (do one pleasant thing today) and long-term (build a life worth living)
- Build mastery: do one thing daily that creates a sense of competence and achievement
- Cope ahead: rehearse coping with difficult situations before they happen
- treat PhysicaL illness: care for your body
- balanced Eating
- Avoid mood-altering substances
- balanced Sleep
- Exercise daily

Opposite Action:
When an emotion is unjustified or ineffective, act opposite to the action urge.
- Fear urges avoidance → approach the feared situation
- Shame urges hiding → share the experience with a trusted person
- Anger urges attack → be kind, gently avoid, take space
- Sadness/depression urges withdrawal → engage, be active, approach
- Guilt (justified) → repair, apologize, make amends

Check the Facts:
Emotions are often driven by interpretations, not facts. Ask: What is the situation exactly? What am I assuming? What is the evidence? Is my interpretation the only one possible?

Opposite to Emotion Action:
Identify the emotion, identify the action urge, act opposite. Repeat until the emotion shifts.

Problem-Solving:
When the emotion is justified and the situation can be changed, solve the problem rather than just regulate the emotion.

INTERPERSONAL EFFECTIVENESS SKILLS

DEAR MAN (getting what you want while maintaining self-respect):
- Describe: describe the situation factually
- Express: express how you feel about it ("I feel...")
- Assert: ask clearly for what you want or say no clearly
- Reinforce: explain how meeting your request benefits them
- Mindful: stay focused on your goal, ignore distractions or attacks
- Appear confident: use confident body language and tone even if you don't feel it
- Negotiate: be willing to give to get; offer alternatives

GIVE (maintaining relationships):
- Gentle: no attacks, threats, or judgments
- Interested: listen and appear interested in what the other person says
- Validate: acknowledge the other person's feelings and perspective
- Easy manner: use humor, smile, be light-hearted when appropriate

FAST (maintaining self-respect):
- Fair: be fair to yourself and the other person
- Apologies: don't over-apologize or apologize for existing
- Stick to values: don't compromise your values for approval
- Truthful: don't lie or exaggerate

THE BIOSOCIAL THEORY (understanding emotional dysregulation)

DBT proposes that emotional dysregulation results from biological sensitivity combined with an invalidating environment. Biological sensitivity means some people are born with a more reactive emotional system — emotions arise faster, more intensely, and take longer to return to baseline. An invalidating environment repeatedly communicates that a person's emotions and perceptions are wrong, bad, or inappropriate. Together, these create chronic emotional dysregulation.

Understanding this reduces self-blame: dysregulation is not a character flaw but a learned response to a real set of experiences and a real biological makeup.

VALIDATION

Validation is a core DBT skill for both therapists and clients in interpersonal situations. It means communicating that a person's feelings, thoughts, or behaviors make sense given their history and current situation.

Levels of validation:
1. Pay attention — listen and be present
2. Accurate reflection — reflect back what was said
3. Mind reading — articulate what the person hasn't said but seems to feel
4. Validate based on history — "given what you've been through, it makes sense"
5. Validate in the present — "anyone in this situation would feel this way"
6. Radical genuineness — treat the person as capable and worthy
