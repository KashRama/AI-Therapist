Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT)

ACT (pronounced as one word, "act") is a third-wave behavioral therapy developed by Steven Hayes. Rather than reducing or eliminating difficult thoughts and feelings, ACT teaches people to change their relationship with them. The goal is psychological flexibility: the ability to be in contact with the present moment fully, and to act in line with values even when difficult thoughts and feelings are present.

The ACT model has six core processes, often visualized as a hexagon (the "hexaflex").

---

THE SIX CORE PROCESSES

1. ACCEPTANCE
Acceptance means opening up and making room for difficult feelings, sensations, and thoughts rather than fighting them. Struggling against painful inner experiences often amplifies them and takes up enormous energy. Acceptance is not resignation — it is willingness to experience discomfort in service of a meaningful life.

Practice: When a difficult feeling arises, instead of suppressing or fighting it, try saying internally: "I notice I am feeling [emotion]. I can make room for this feeling. It doesn't have to go away for me to act on what matters."

2. COGNITIVE DEFUSION
Defusion means stepping back from thoughts and seeing them as mental events rather than facts or commands. Fused thinking: "I am worthless" (believed as truth). Defused thinking: "I notice I'm having the thought that I am worthless."

Defusion techniques:
- Prefix thoughts with "I'm having the thought that..."
- Name the story: "There goes my 'I'm a failure' story again"
- Thank your mind: "Thanks, mind, for that thought"
- Leaves on a stream: imagine thoughts as leaves floating by on a stream; observe without grabbing them
- The computer screen: imagine the thought on a screen — change its font, color, or speed

3. PRESENT MOMENT AWARENESS
Flexible attention to the here and now. Much of psychological suffering involves living in the past (rumination) or future (worry). Present moment awareness means noticing what is happening right now with openness and curiosity.

Practice: Pause and notice five things you can observe right now with each sense. Or simply ask: "What is happening in my experience right now — in my body, thoughts, and feelings?"

4. SELF-AS-CONTEXT (The Observing Self)
There are two parts of the self: the thinking self (the ongoing stream of thoughts, judgments, stories) and the observing self (the part that notices thoughts and feelings, sometimes called the "noticing self" or "sky mind"). The observing self cannot be damaged or diminished — it is the stable perspective from which experience is witnessed.

Metaphor: You are the sky, not the weather. Storms (difficult emotions, thoughts) pass through, but the sky itself is unchanged.

Practice: "I am the one who notices this feeling. The feeling is in me, but it is not me."

5. VALUES
Values are chosen directions for living — what matters most, what kind of person you want to be. They are not goals (which can be completed) but ongoing directions like "being kind," "being present with family," or "contributing to others." Clarifying values gives purpose and direction.

Values clarification questions:
- If no one would judge you and you couldn't fail, what would you do with your life?
- What do you want to stand for?
- When you are at your best, what qualities are you expressing?
- Looking back at the end of your life, what would you most want to have done?

Common values domains: relationships, parenting, friendship, work, education, health, community, spirituality, creativity, leisure.

6. COMMITTED ACTION
Taking steps toward values, even in the presence of obstacles including difficult thoughts and feelings. This involves setting goals consistent with values and following through, using defusion and acceptance when barriers arise.

The ACT question: "Is what I'm doing right now taking me toward or away from the life I want?"

---

PSYCHOLOGICAL FLEXIBILITY

The six processes work together to create psychological flexibility: the ability to be present, open up to difficult experience, and do what matters. Rigidity — fusing with thoughts, avoiding feelings, living on autopilot, or losing contact with values — creates suffering.

---

THE STRUGGLE SWITCH METAPHOR

Imagine your emotions have a "struggle switch." When it is on, uncomfortable feelings are treated as problems to be eliminated. The struggle against the feeling creates a second layer of suffering: anxiety about anxiety, sadness about being sad. When the switch is off, feelings are simply experiences to be noticed — they rise and fall naturally without adding a second layer of struggle.

---

MINDFULNESS IN ACT

ACT uses mindfulness not as a relaxation technique but as a way to build psychological flexibility. The goal is not to feel calm — it is to be fully present with whatever arises, and to act effectively from that place.

A short ACT mindfulness practice:
1. Notice what is happening right now in your body (take 30 seconds)
2. Notice what your mind is telling you — label the thoughts without believing or fighting them
3. Ask: what matters to me right now? What is one small step I can take?

---

ACT FOR COMMON PROBLEMS

For anxiety: the aim is not to reduce anxiety but to stop letting it control behavior. Values-based action continues even when anxious.

For depression: behavioral activation guided by values rather than mood. "What would I do today if my values were in charge, not my depression?"

For chronic pain: acceptance of pain as a sensation, defusion from catastrophic thoughts about pain, living fully despite pain.

For grief: making room for grief rather than suppressing it, while continuing to engage with life and values.
