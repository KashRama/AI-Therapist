Trauma and Grief: Understanding and Healing

---

TRAUMA

Trauma is the lasting emotional, psychological, and physiological response to a deeply distressing event or series of events that overwhelms a person's ability to cope. Trauma is defined not by the event itself but by the impact on the person.

Types of trauma:
- Acute trauma: a single overwhelming event (accident, assault, natural disaster)
- Chronic/complex trauma: repeated or prolonged exposure (childhood abuse, domestic violence, war)
- Developmental trauma: trauma occurring during critical periods of child development, affecting the developing nervous system and sense of self
- Secondary/vicarious trauma: developing trauma symptoms from indirect exposure (first responders, therapists, caregivers)
- Collective trauma: shared trauma affecting communities or groups

Common responses to trauma:
- Intrusion: flashbacks, nightmares, intrusive memories, emotional flooding when reminded of the trauma
- Avoidance: avoiding thoughts, feelings, people, places, or activities associated with the trauma
- Negative alterations in cognition and mood: persistent negative beliefs ("I am broken," "the world is completely dangerous"), distorted self-blame, emotional numbing, estrangement from others, loss of interest
- Hyperarousal: hypervigilance, exaggerated startle response, difficulty sleeping, irritability, concentration problems

The nervous system and trauma:
Trauma dysregulates the autonomic nervous system. Survivors may get stuck in states of hyperactivation (fight/flight: anxiety, panic, aggression) or hypoactivation (freeze/collapse: numbness, dissociation, depression). Healing involves restoring the capacity to move flexibly between activation and rest.

---

TRAUMA-INFORMED PRINCIPLES

Any supportive interaction with someone who has experienced trauma should be guided by:

Safety: Creating physical and emotional safety. Survivors need to feel that the environment and the relationship are safe before processing can occur.

Trustworthiness: Being transparent, consistent, and following through on commitments.

Peer support: Connection with others who have shared experiences reduces shame and isolation.

Collaboration: Trauma survivors often experienced powerlessness. Sharing power and decision-making in the helping relationship counteracts this.

Empowerment: Supporting the person's own strengths and coping rather than positioning them as a passive recipient of help.

Cultural humility: Recognizing how culture, race, gender, and identity shape both trauma experiences and healing.

Avoiding retraumatization: Be careful not to recreate dynamics of powerlessness, shame, or violation.

---

TRAUMA-FOCUSED TREATMENTS

Trauma-Focused Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (TF-CBT):
Combines trauma processing with cognitive and behavioral skills. Originally developed for children but adapted for adults. Components include psychoeducation, relaxation, affective regulation, cognitive coping, trauma narrative development, and in-vivo mastery of trauma reminders.

EMDR (Eye Movement Desensitization and Reprocessing):
Developed by Francine Shapiro. Involves recalling traumatic memories while following bilateral stimulation (therapist's finger movements, taps, or tones). Believed to mimic the processing that occurs during REM sleep. Reduces the emotional charge of traumatic memories. Strong research support.

Prolonged Exposure (PE):
Developed by Edna Foa. Involves imaginal exposure (recounting the trauma narrative repeatedly in session until distress decreases) and in-vivo exposure (gradually approaching avoided situations). Highly effective for PTSD.

Somatic approaches:
Trauma is stored in the body as well as the mind. Somatic therapies (Somatic Experiencing, Sensorimotor Psychotherapy) work directly with physical sensations, movement, and body awareness to release trauma held in the nervous system without necessarily talking through the narrative.

Stabilization before processing:
For complex trauma, establishing safety and emotional regulation skills before processing traumatic memories is essential. Processing too quickly can be destabilizing. Skills from DBT and other modalities are often used in this stabilization phase.

GRIEF

Grief is the natural response to loss — not only the death of a loved one, but any significant loss: a relationship, a job, health, a sense of safety, a future that was expected. Grief is not a pathology but a necessary process.

The Myth of Stages:
Kübler-Ross's five stages (denial, anger, bargaining, depression, acceptance) were originally described in the context of people receiving terminal diagnoses, not bereaved people. Grief does not follow a predictable sequence. People cycle in and out of different experiences, sometimes moment to moment.

The Dual Process Model (Stroebe and Schut):
A more current model proposes that bereaved people oscillate between:
- Loss orientation: confronting and processing the grief (crying, thinking about the person, longing)
- Restoration orientation: taking breaks from grief, adjusting to life without the loss, attending to secondary changes

Healthy grieving involves moving between these two, not staying stuck in either.

Tasks of Mourning (William Worden):
1. Accept the reality of the loss
2. Work through the pain of grief
3. Adjust to a world without the deceased (practically, emotionally, existentially)
4. Find an enduring connection with the deceased while embarking on a new life

Complicated grief (prolonged grief disorder):
When grief is intense and persistent well beyond the expected period, causing significant functional impairment, it may be complicated grief. Features include: intense longing, difficulty accepting the death, bitterness or anger about the loss, difficulty imagining meaningful life without the person, and social withdrawal. Treatment includes grief-focused therapy and careful processing of the loss.

---

WHAT HELPS IN GRIEF

Presence: Simply being with someone in their grief, without trying to fix it or offer silver linings.

Validation: "This is devastating." "Missing them makes complete sense." Grief does not need to be talked out of.

Allowing the process: Grief comes in waves. Suppression prolongs it. Creating space to cry, remember, and feel is part of healing.

Continuing bonds: Research challenges the idea that "moving on" means detaching from the deceased. Maintaining an internal relationship with the person — talking to them, honoring them, carrying their values forward — is healthy and common.

Meaning-making: In time, many bereaved people find meaning in their loss: changed values, deeper relationships, new purpose. This cannot be rushed and should never be imposed.

Social support: Isolation worsens grief. Even imperfect social support is helpful. Grief groups can be powerful because they reduce the isolation of loss.

Physical self-care: Grief is physically exhausting. Sleep, nutrition, movement, and gentle self-care support the body's capacity to process.

Professional support: If grief significantly impairs functioning, involves thoughts of suicide, or is stuck in complicated patterns, a grief-informed therapist can help.
